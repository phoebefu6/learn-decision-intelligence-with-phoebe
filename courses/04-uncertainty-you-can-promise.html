LiveTwo kinds of 90 percent3 min▶

After tonight the memo can carry two different creatures that both say "90":

  • The human's trained 90 (session 2): "my 90 percent intervals capture the truth about 90 percent of the time - measured, on my own scored history." Certified by a hit rate.
  • The algorithm's guaranteed 90 (tonight): "this prediction set contains the true label with 90 percent probability - guaranteed by construction, under stated conditions." Certified by a theorem.

They are not interchangeable, and the memo should never let them blur: the human number covers one-off judgment calls no model scores; the guaranteed number covers repeated, model-scored predictions. The memo carries both - labeled, so the reader always knows which certificate stands behind which number.

Self-studyTonight's vocabulary3 min▶
  • Calibration curve - stated confidence on one axis, actual frequency on the other. The diagonal is honesty; session 2 drew yours, tonight draws the model's.
  • Calibration set - labeled points the model never trained on, held out to price its errors.
  • Nonconformity score - how "wrong" the model looks on one point, e.g. 1 minus the probability it gave the true label. High score = the truth surprised it.
  • Coverage - how often the prediction set actually contains the truth. The thing being guaranteed.
  • Exchangeability - the condition under which the guarantee holds: calibration data and new data behave like draws from the same well-shuffled deck. Distribution shift breaks it.
Part 1 · covers calibration curves for models

Audit the model like you audited yourself

6 min live

Session 2's lab bucketed YOUR stated confidence and plotted your actual hit rate. The exact same audit works on a model's probability outputs: bucket its predictions by stated confidence, plot the actual frequency in each bucket, and compare to the diagonal. On the diagonal, the model is calibrated. Churn models routinely are not - they run over- or under-confident, and nobody notices until the curve is drawn.

LiveHow the audit works, and what it finds3 min▶

Three moves, all bookkeeping: take a batch of scored predictions with known outcomes, group them into confidence buckets (all the "about 80%" predictions together), and compute the actual positive rate inside each bucket. Plot stated against actual. A calibrated model sits on the diagonal; the sagging curve above shows the common failure - the high-confidence buckets are where overconfidence concentrates, exactly as it did in the human lab.

The connection to session 2 is the point: a model's "84% churn risk" is a confidence claim from an estimator, and it deserves the same audit a colleague's "90% sure" got. Nobody at Mango Lane had ever bucketed the churn model's outputs. The curve is an afternoon of work and it changes what the number is worth in the memo.

Real world

Mango Lane's churn model flags a segment at "84% risk". Bucketed against last quarter's outcomes, the 80-90 bucket actually churned at 71%. The score is still useful - it ranks customers well - but the memo cannot write "84% probability" with a straight face. Tonight's second tool fixes that properly: instead of hoping the probabilities are honest, wrap them in a set that carries a guarantee.

Self-studyWhy good models are often badly calibrated3 min▶

Ranking well and stating honest probabilities are different skills. A model can order customers from safest to riskiest almost perfectly while its stated probabilities run systematically hot or cold - training objectives reward separation, not honesty, and modern flexible models tend toward overconfidence at the extremes. Two practical consequences:

  • Never read a raw score as a probability until the calibration curve has been drawn on held-out data. The curve is the model's session-2 lab result.
  • Calibration drifts. A curve drawn last year certifies last year. Re-audit on a cadence, and treat a sudden sag as an early warning that the world the model learned has moved - which is exactly the condition that voids tonight's guarantee too.
Part 2 · covers conformal prediction

The guarantee: distribution-free, by construction

7 min live

Calibration curves diagnose; conformal prediction certifies. Instead of trusting the model's probabilities, it outputs a prediction SET - possibly one label, possibly several - built so that the set contains the truth at a rate you chose in advance. The remarkable part is what it does NOT need: no assumptions about the data's distribution, no assumptions about the model.

Verbatim · Angelopoulos & Bates 2021Conformal prediction sets "are valid in a distribution-free sense: they possess explicit, non-asymptotic guarantees even without distributional assumptions or model assumptions." The method works "with any pre-trained model, such as a neural network, to produce sets that are guaranteed to contain the ground truth with a user-specified probability, such as 90%."
LiveThe recipe in plain words4 min▶

Split conformal is four moves, and only one involves any math (paraphrasing the Angelopoulos-Bates tutorial):

  • Hold out a calibration set - labeled points the model never saw in training. This is the model's exam, not its homework.
  • Compute a nonconformity score per calibration point - a number for how wrong the model looks there, e.g. 1 minus the probability it assigned the true label.
  • Take the ceil((n+1)(1-alpha))/n empirical quantile of those scores - for 90% coverage, alpha is 0.1. This threshold is where the guarantee is manufactured: the (n+1) is a finite-sample correction, not a typo.
  • Build the prediction set for a new customer: every candidate label whose score falls below the threshold goes in.

That is the whole method - a few lines on top of ANY model, including the gradient booster you built in the ensemble course. Uncertain customers naturally get bigger sets, confident ones get singletons; the set SIZE becomes an honest per-customer uncertainty readout even though the guarantee itself is an average.

LiveThe fine print is content, not a footnote3 min▶

Two conditions bound the promise, and the memo must carry both:

  • Marginal, not conditional. Coverage holds ON AVERAGE over exchangeable data - roughly 90% of customers get a set containing their truth. It is NOT a per-instance promise: no individual customer is personally guaranteed 90%, and coverage can run lower on some slices while the average still holds.
  • Exchangeability, not forever. The theorem assumes calibration data and new data are exchangeable - same well-shuffled deck. Distribution shift breaks that: a drifted quarter voids the promise outright.

The second condition is not a weakness to whisper - it is a reason to use session 1's machinery. The memo's flip-trigger should name it: "these sets are guaranteed under stable conditions; if the input distribution shifts materially, recalibrate before trusting them." A guarantee with stated conditions is a promise; one with hidden conditions is a liability.

Build-along

Build a conformal set by hand

★ 20 min · everyone builds

No library, no notebook - ten numbers and a pencil, because the quantile move is the entire method and doing it once by hand makes the guarantee stop being magic. Mango Lane's churn model, ten held-out customers, their nonconformity scores already computed (1 minus the model's probability on each customer's true label), already sorted: 0.02, 0.05, 0.08, 0.11, 0.15, 0.19, 0.24, 0.31, 0.42, 0.55.

Sort the calibration scores. Done for you above - ten scores, ascending. Each is one held-out customer: low score means the model gave the true label high probability (no surprise); 0.55 means the truth caught the model fairly flat-footed. The threshold you are about to pick comes from these ten exam results.

Compute the quantile for 90% coverage at n = 10. The recipe says take the ceil((n+1)(1-alpha))/n quantile: ceil(11 x 0.9)/10 = ceil(9.9)/10 = 10/10 - the 100th percentile, meaning the 10th and largest value: threshold = 0.55. Feel the finite-sample bump: with only 10 points, the guarantee demands the MAXIMUM score as the threshold. Small calibration sets buy wide promises - the honesty tax on tiny n.

Redo it at n = 19 and watch the tax shrink. ceil((19+1) x 0.9)/19 = ceil(18)/19 = 18/19 - the 18th of 19 sorted scores, comfortably below the maximum. Nine extra calibration points move the threshold off the worst case; a few hundred make it tight. Rule of thumb from the arithmetic: the calibration set is cheap coverage insurance - grow it.

Apply the threshold to a new customer. A candidate label for tonight's new customer scores 0.30. At the n = 10 threshold of 0.55: 0.30 < 0.55, so the label goes IN the prediction set. Now write the promise sentence exactly as the memo will carry it: "sets built this way contain the true label for 90% of customers, on average, under conditions exchangeable with calibration."

Upgrade the memo's estimates section. Go row by row: campaign uptake and any one-off judgment number stays a calibrated human interval (session 2's grade, hit rate cited). Every repeated model-scored prediction - the churn calls feeding the voucher case - becomes a conformal set with the stable-conditions caveat attached in the same sentence, and the flip-trigger gains a line: "material input drift voids the coverage guarantee; recalibrate before acting on the sets."

What to bring to session 5. Your memo with estimates now graded and labeled. Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The memo says the churn sets are "guaranteed to contain the truth 90% of the time, distribution-free". What is that guarantee actually promising?

Distribution-free means no assumptions on the data's distribution or the model - not "no conditions at all". The guarantee is MARGINAL coverage under exchangeability: right on average, silent per instance, and void under shift - which is why the memo's flip-trigger names drift explicitly.

2 · Ten sorted calibration scores, target coverage 90%. Which value becomes the threshold?

The (n+1) finite-sample correction is the whole trick: ceil(11 x 0.9) = ceil(9.9) = 10, so at n = 10 the guarantee demands the max. That is not a flaw - it is the price of an honest promise from ten data points. At n = 19 the threshold relaxes to the 18th of 19; more calibration data buys tighter sets.

3 · The memo has three numbers: campaign uptake (a one-off judgment call), churn per customer (model-scored, repeated), and "the segment feels risky". Which uncertainty grade belongs to each?

Conformal needs a model and repeated scored predictions - it cannot wrap a one-off judgment call, which is what the calibrated human stays for. The three grades coexist in one memo; the discipline is labeling which certificate stands behind which number, so a reader never mistakes a vibe for a guarantee.
